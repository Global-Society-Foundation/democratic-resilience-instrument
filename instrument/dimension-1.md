# Dimension 1: Media and Political Literacy

*Cognitive/epistemic dimension. Covers knowledge of democratic institutions and processes, perceived civic self-efficacy, the ability to identify mis- and disinformation, understanding of political complexity and trade-offs, and understanding of the media ecosystem.*

| | |
|---|---|
| **Two forms** | Form A and Form B are parallel forms measuring the same constructs in different words. Form order is randomised per participant and fixed across two waves, pre- and post-treatment. |
| **Two levels** | Each construct can be tested for national-level or EU-level programmes. |
| **Response scale** | 0–1, via knowledge tests and scenario-based items, and 5-point agree/disagree Likert scale items. |
| **Measurement model** | Formative — the five constructs are distinct components, not interchangeable indicators. |

Administration rules are in [`methodology/administration-and-validation.md`](../methodology/administration-and-validation.md). Do not field these items without reading it.

## 1.1 Knowledge of democratic institutions and processes

### 1.1.1 — National level · scale 0–1

**Form A.** In a parliamentary democracy, if the government loses a vote of no confidence, what typically happens?

1. The government must resign or call new elections. **(correct)**
2. The parliament must be re-elected.
3. The vote has no formal consequence.
4. The courts annul the most recently passed legislation.

**Form B.** What is the primary function of a constitutional court in a democracy?

1. To review whether laws and government actions comply with the constitution. **(correct)**
2. To draft new legislation jointly with the parliament.
3. To manage elections and certify their results.
4. To represent the country in international courts.

### 1.1.2 — EU level · scale 0–1

**Form A.** The European Parliament can force the entire European Commission to resign through a formal vote. What is this mechanism called?

1. A motion of censure. **(correct)**
2. A vote of no confidence.
3. A qualified majority vote.
4. An Article 7 procedure.

**Form B.** What is the primary function of the Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU)?

1. To ensure that EU law is interpreted and applied consistently across all member states. **(correct)**
2. To draft new EU legislation jointly with the European Parliament.
3. To manage European Parliament elections and certify their results.
4. To represent the EU in trade negotiations with non-EU countries.

## 1.3 Ability to identify mis- and disinformation

> Building on: Roozenbeek, J., & van der Linden, S. (2019). *Fake news game confers psychological resistance against online misinformation.* Humanities and Social Sciences Communications, 5, 65.

### 1.3.1 — National level · scale 0–1

**Form A.** A national food safety agency reports that 4% of imported vegetables contain more pesticide than the law allows. A social media post about this report says: *"Poison on your plate. Think twice before you feed this to your children tonight."* Which logical fallacy is most clearly being used in the post?

1. Emotional language: using words chosen to make you feel something strongly, instead of just giving you the facts. **(correct)**
2. Conspiracy: claiming that a group is secretly working together to do harm, without showing proof.
3. Discrediting: attacking the people behind the information, instead of what they actually found.
4. Impersonation: making something look like it came from an official source when it did not.

**Form B.** A social media post says: *"An adviser told us that after a private meeting, the Prime Minister said elections are out of date and the results do not really matter any more."* The adviser is not named, and the meeting was private. Which logical fallacy is most clearly being used in the post?

1. Unverified attribution: saying that someone said something, without any way to check it. **(correct)**
2. Emotional language: using words chosen to make you feel something strongly, instead of just giving you the facts.
3. Discrediting: attacking the people behind the information, instead of what they actually found.
4. Impersonation: making something look like it came from an official source when it did not.

### 1.3.2 — EU level · scale 0–1

**Form A.** A report finds that a set of EU rules is working well. A social media post about the report says: *"Look who wrote it. These are Brussels officials. Their own departments would lose money if the rules were found not to work."* Which logical fallacy is most clearly being used in the post?

1. Discrediting: attacking the people behind the information, instead of what they actually found. **(correct)**
2. Conspiracy: claiming that a group is secretly working together to do harm, without showing proof.
3. Emotional language: using words chosen to make you feel something strongly, instead of just giving you the facts.
4. Unverified attribution: saying that someone said something, without any way to check it.

**Form B.** A social media post says: *"For years, a small group of officials in Brussels has worked behind the scenes to make the rules suit a few big companies, and to push smaller companies out."* No details are given. Which logical fallacy is most clearly being used in the post?

1. Conspiracy: claiming that a group is secretly working together to do harm, without showing proof. **(correct)**
2. Discrediting: attacking the people behind the information, instead of what they actually found.
3. Polarisation: describing things so that two groups are pushed further apart.
4. Emotional language: using words chosen to make you feel something strongly, instead of just giving you the facts.

## 1.4 Understanding of political complexity and trade-offs

### 1.4.1 — National level · scale 0–1

**Form A.** The government wants to reduce hospital waiting times. It can hire more staff, which would mean raising taxes, or it can leave things as they are. Which of these is true?

1. Both choices have a cost: one costs taxpayers money, the other costs patients time. **(correct)**
2. Only hiring more staff has a cost, because leaving things as they are costs nothing.
3. Only leaving things as they are has a cost, because higher taxes always pay for themselves in the end.
4. Neither has a real cost, because the money can always be found by cutting waste, with no effect on anything else.

**Form B.** The government wants to raise the minimum wage. Supporters say the lowest-paid workers would earn more. Opponents say some small businesses would have to cut jobs to afford it. Which of these is true?

1. If the change goes ahead, some people are likely to gain and others may lose. **(correct)**
2. If the change goes ahead, nobody loses anything.
3. Raising the minimum wage always costs jobs, so there is nothing to weigh up.
4. There is no real disagreement or trade-off here.

### 1.4.2 — EU level · scale 0–1

**Form A.** The EU wants to build up a shared stockpile of medicines for future health emergencies. Paying for it would mean that member states have to contribute more taxes, or that the EU spends less on other programmes. Which of these is true?

1. Both choices have a cost: one costs member countries money, the other leaves the EU less prepared for the next emergency. **(correct)**
2. Only building the stockpile has a cost, because leaving things as they are costs nothing.
3. Only leaving things as they are has a cost, because the stockpile would pay for itself.
4. Neither has a real cost, because the money can always be found by cutting waste, with no effect on anything else.

**Form B.** The EU wants stricter limits on pollution from steel and cement factories. Supporters say this protects people's health. Opponents say some factories would move abroad, taking jobs away. Which of these is true?

1. If the limits go ahead, some people are likely to gain and others may lose. **(correct)**
2. If the limits go ahead, nobody loses anything.
3. Stricter limits always cost jobs, so there is nothing to weigh up.
4. There is no real disagreement or trade-off here.

## 1.5 Understanding of the media ecosystem

### 1.5.1 — National level · scale 0–1

**Form A.** Why are shocking or emotional headlines more common on social media than in printed newspapers?

1. Social media shows people whatever keeps them looking, and shocking posts do that best. **(correct)**
2. Social media has no editors, so nothing stops anyone posting.
3. Newspapers are just as shocking; people only think social media is worse.
4. People now know how social media picks posts, and that makes them want shocking ones.

**Form B.** Some problems affect many people but rarely appear in the news. Why?

1. News tends to cover what is new or unusual, and slow, steady problems are neither. **(correct)**
2. Journalists are told by media owners which subjects to avoid.
3. If something is important enough, it will always be reported eventually.
4. There is no pattern, what appears in the news is mostly random.

### 1.5.2 — EU level · scale 0–1

**Form A.** EU law now makes big platforms explain how they choose which posts to show. Even so, shocking headlines are still common. Why?

1. Explaining how the system works does not change what it is built to do: keep people looking. **(correct)**
2. The law applies only to newspapers and television, not to social media.
3. Shocking headlines have actually become rare since the law.
4. Platforms in the EU have no editors, so nothing stops anyone posting.

**Form B.** News about the EU is mostly reported when there is a crisis or a disagreement. Why?

1. News tends to cover what is new or dramatic, and routine decision-making is neither. **(correct)**
2. EU institutions prevent journalists from reporting on ordinary business.
3. Very little actually happens in the EU between crises.
4. EU rules require journalists to report only on decisions that are already final.
